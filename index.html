Learning When to Adapt: Reactivity-Conditioned Residual Adaptation under Human–Robot Interaction Shift

RCRA adapts robot crowd navigation to estimated pedestrian responses by modulating a learned residual around a frozen conservative policy.

Abstract

Pedestrians differ in whether they respond to a mobile robot, and these responses can change during navigation. A policy trained under a single response assumption may therefore take unnecessary detours or rely on cooperation that does not occur. We propose Reactivity-Conditioned Residual Adaptation (RCRA), which uses explicit estimates of pedestrian reactivity to adjust a pretrained navigation policy. A Transformer estimates each pedestrian's reactivity from motion history and robot context. These estimates control a learned low-rank residual around a frozen conservative backbone, enabling online adaptation without gradient updates. Across five simulated interaction scenarios, RCRA reduces average navigation time by 16.8% and path length by 16.7% relative to the conservative backbone, with mean success rates of 96.05% and 98.35%, respectively. Evaluation on recorded human trajectories and controlled robot trials further examines reactivity estimation and residual adaptation.

Overview of the RCRA Framework

RCRA connects pedestrian reactivity estimation with continuous policy adaptation. The robot estimates how nearby pedestrians respond to its motion and uses these estimates to determine how strongly to activate a residual learned from reactive crowds.

Motion history and robot context provide per-pedestrian reactivity estimates. Spatial aggregation converts these estimates into a continuous scale that modulates the learned residual around the frozen conservative backbone.

Estimating Pedestrian Reactivity

A Transformer processes human motion history and robot context to estimate the probability that each pedestrian responds to the robot. The estimator is trained using simulator labels and refined on observations collected by the adaptive policy. It does not require intended pedestrian goals.

Prioritizing Local Interactions

Reactivity estimates are weighted by pedestrian distance and bearing, emphasizing nearby pedestrians ahead of the robot. This gives a nearby non-reactive pedestrian greater influence than a distant pedestrian at the same bearing. The weighted estimates determine a continuous adaptation scale.

Modulating the Navigation Policy

A conservative policy is first trained with non-reactive pedestrians and then frozen. Low-rank residual modules are learned from reactive crowds. During navigation, the adaptation scale controls these residuals, allowing the robot to adjust its behavior as local interactions change. All learned parameters remain fixed during deployment.

Results from Crowd Navigation Simulations

We evaluate RCRA with 20 pedestrians in a 12 m × 12 m CrowdNav environment. Five scenarios vary pedestrian responses across people, locations, and time: Non-reactive, Reactive, Mixed, Spatial Clusters, and Time-varying.

Navigation in Mixed Crowds

This comparison illustrates how different policies handle reactive and non-reactive pedestrians within the same scene. The conservative policy takes a long detour, while the naive policy follows a direct route but collides with a non-reactive pedestrian. RCRA adjusts its residual strength as local interactions change, using opportunities created by reactive pedestrians while becoming more conservative near a non-reactive pedestrian.

Performance Across Interaction Scenarios

Across the five scenarios, RCRA reduces average navigation time by 16.8% and path length by 16.7% relative to GenSafeNav (Cons.), while mean success rate decreases from 98.35% to 96.05%. In fully reactive crowds, navigation time and path length decrease by approximately 32%, with a 99.28% success rate. These results demonstrate the empirical trade-off between navigation efficiency and success.

Real-Robot Experiments

We validate residual adaptation on a Clearpath Jackal navigating through four pedestrians: two instructed to maintain their nominal motion and two instructed to respond to the robot. The experiment uses prescribed reactivity labels to isolate the adaptation mechanism.

In the representative comparison, the conservative policy takes a long detour and the naive policy collides with a non-reactive pedestrian. RCRA yields near that pedestrian while using the gap created by reactive pedestrians. Across 20 trials per policy, RCRA completes all 20 runs successfully, compared with 16 for the naive policy. Relative to the conservative policy, RCRA reduces navigation time by 15.4% and path length by 26.5%.

Reactivity Estimation from Real Human Motion

We evaluate the estimator on recorded moving-robot trajectories from PeRoI, using avoidance and neutrality annotations as proxies for reactive and non-reactive behavior. The estimator achieves 73.0% balanced accuracy, compared with 68.8% for the tuned Behavior-Driven Danger Signaling (BDDS) baseline. This offline evaluation supports learning reactivity cues from observed human motion. Evaluating the learned estimator in closed-loop real-robot navigation remains future work.
